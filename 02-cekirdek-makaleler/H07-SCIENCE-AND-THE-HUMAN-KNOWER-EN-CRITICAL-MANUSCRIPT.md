# Science and the Human Knower: Being, Creative Knowledge, and the Second-Knower Thesis

**H07 / BİLİM-01 — English critical manuscript, working version 0.1 (22 October 2026)**

**Status:** A unified, source-grounded **critical reconstruction**, not a certified complete translation, independently peer-reviewed scientific theory, or journal-ready paper. The historical author's propositions are distinguished from editorial assessment. No bibliography is fabricated.

**Primary historical source:** `veysieken000-ctrl/hebun-zanabun`, `main`, `articles/makale-06.md`, “VARLIK VE YARATICI BİLİMİNİN TEMELİ / BİLİM VE İNSAN,” lines 1–129. English paragraph renderings and scientific audit: `BILIM-01-EPISTEMIK-STATU-VE-KANIT-MATRISI.md` on this branch.

## Abstract

The historical manuscript presents an account of science grounded in a relation among a knowing subject, a known object, and a second knower who confirms the result. It distinguishes the *science of being*, concerned with existing things and their interactions, from *creative science*, concerned with rationally combining knowledge and materials to produce new human artifacts. The argument extends from physical, biological, and mental organization to law, morality, and an eschatological account of judgment and life. This critical reconstruction identifies the proposal's central epistemic insight—the need to distinguish claims from shared, independently assessable realities—while marking the limits of agreement as evidence and separating metaphysical premises from empirical findings. Its purpose is to establish a transparent text for subsequent translation verification and scholarly review, not to assert that the model has been experimentally validated.

**Keywords:** epistemology; second knower; science of being; creative knowledge; ontology; intersubjective confirmation; Zanabûn; Hebûn; philosophy of science.

## 1. Introduction: science and human existence

The original begins with human beings as social living creatures seeking to know nature, adapt to it, and sustain life. Impressions acquired between birth and death are treated as information for individuals, while social knowledge is characterized as a form of agreement reached through shared efforts to live (source lines 6–12). The author defines knowledge in terms of a knowing subject's orientation toward a known object and the resulting product. Science emerges through the classification, accumulation, and scrutiny of such knowledge.

Human cultures transmit knowledge through discoveries, traditions, books, inscriptions, tools, and historical symbols (lines 21–23). The source defines science as an orderly and consistent way of investigating events, drawing on method, experiment, and reality; it associates *ilim* with systematic knowledge confirmed in application (lines 25–26). These are authorial definitions, not claims of universal terminological agreement.

## 2. The epistemic triad: first knower, second knower, and independent reality

The manuscript proposes that knowledge requires a knowing subject, and that a second knower's confirmation enables knowledge to acquire shared and continuing standing. Two persons who affirm a claim must also encounter a reality that does not depend simply on their agreement. Reason is described as their meeting ground, while the known object is the third term against which their claims can be considered (line 27).

This framework can be read as a proposal about intersubjective scrutiny. Its strongest defensible methodological formulation is that claims should be publicly intelligible, open to independent checking, and responsive to evidence. Yet agreement between two observers does not establish truth by itself: both may rely on the same mistaken premise, instrument, or method. The original goes further by inferring a universal knower from universal law. That inference belongs to its philosophical-theological argument and is not presented here as an experimental result.

## 3. Science of being: physical, biological, and mental orders

The author reconstructs existence through a first knowing subject, a second knower, a known product, and a common ordering or law (lines 33–43). Creation, spirit, and divine agency are introduced as metaphysical premises. The account defines being by center and periphery, an internal triad, and an ordering law. These are conceptual organizing analogies, not demonstrated structural properties of every entity.

The **physical order** is illustrated by particles, atoms, stars, planets, and gravitation (line 45). The **biological order** is illustrated by cells and the interplay of DNA, RNA, and proteins (line 47). The **mental order** is illustrated by nervous systems, sensation, memory, intelligence, and reason (line 49). The manuscript then proposes spirit, innate disposition (*fıtrat*), natural law, and angels as higher-order ordering principles (lines 51–55).

Several physical and biological descriptions in the historical manuscript are incorrect by current scientific standards. For example, hydrogen-1 has no neutron, helium has two protons, and sexual reproduction is not equivalent to multicellularity. A critical edition must preserve the historical author's claims as textual evidence without treating them as verified scientific explanations. The later founder-approved biological stages are **unicellular** and **multicellular** life; these are a canonical revision, not a literal replacement for line 47.

## 4. Methods, knowledge accumulation, and the intention to know

The manuscript associates scientific inquiry with verification, induction, deduction, and falsifiability, and it emphasizes the question of whether the subject of a claim exists (lines 57–65). It uses mathematics, writing systems, and technological artifacts as examples of knowledge made shareable. Its proposed general pattern is that interaction among subjects gives rise to a product that can be recognized and used by others.

For the author, the science of being also begins with the intention to ask how things came to be. Human beings acquire knowledge to sustain themselves, protect their sphere of existence, and extend it. Knowledge accumulation leads toward general rules; rational combination of existing parts according to those rules leads toward creative production (lines 69–79). This is the crucial transition from knowing what exists to making something new.

The source's claim that all products require at least two people, or that all beings seek to enlarge their sphere of existence, is broader than the evidence supplied. These statements remain philosophical hypotheses requiring definitions and independent tests.

## 5. Six historical modes of time and order

The source says that the universe consists of six modes or periods of time (lines 81–106). It calls them **physical time**, concerning material existence and law; **biological time**, concerning cells and living systems; **mental time**, concerning perception and the processing of information; **time of law**, concerning the ordering of the preceding systems and, in the source's theological interpretation, spirits and angels; **moral time**, concerning human agency, truth, goodness, and responsibility; and **time of life**, concerning ultimate outcomes expressed through paradise and hell.

These six categories are the historical manuscript's philosophical taxonomy. They must not be described as six empirically measured spacetime dimensions. Its assertions that cells are literally two-dimensional, or that physical laws have purposes, require qualification.

The later founder-approved **6+Tek** canon is related but not textually identical: physical stages are atom / stars and planets; biological stages are unicellular / multicellular; mental stages are intelligence / reason; the fourth dimension is spirit / angels; the fifth is innate / volitional morality; the sixth concerns judgment and its outcome in the afterlife. **Tek/Yek** is a unifying principle, not an additional measurable physical axis. This paragraph reports a later interpretive framework, not the wording of the historical source.

## 6. Creative science and the human knower

The source's second main section defines **creative science** as knowledge arising when a human being consciously processes prior knowledge and produces further knowledge (lines 109–113). Human products are offered to others for recognition and use. Everyday, technical, artistic, religious, and other forms of knowledge are included within the author's broad conception of creative production.

The manuscript gives special weight to order: regular outcomes can be known, and creation requires coherent relationships among existing elements (lines 115–117). It also connects knowing with love, moral intention, truthfulness, and goodness. These are normative propositions about responsible creation; they do not imply that every useful artifact or ethical belief has passed a scientific validation procedure.

The author describes the human self as surrounded by feelings, with reason, intelligence, mental processes, biological organization, and physical structure participating in action (lines 119–121). Morality concerns how a person directs these capacities. This is an account of human agency within the author's ontology, not a validated neurobiological model.

## 7. Discussion: a testable program and its limits

The historical manuscript's epistemic contribution is a challenge to make the relation among knower, object, and corroborating observer explicit. A contemporary research program could investigate narrower questions: How does independent replication improve error detection? Which forms of intersubjective agreement remain vulnerable to shared bias? How do artifacts enable knowledge to be transferred, corrected, and accumulated? These are **editorial research questions**, not empirical results already demonstrated by the source.

Its metaphysical commitments—creator, spirit, angels, the afterlife, and a universal purpose of being—should be examined as philosophical or theological claims, with their premises and inferential steps made explicit. They cannot be used to overturn established empirical results merely through analogy. Similarly, the source's objections to evolution do not amount to an empirical refutation of evolutionary biology.

A journal submission will need a specific research question, explicit methodology, documented primary-text passages, engagement with relevant peer-reviewed literature, and a clearly delimited original contribution.

## 8. Conclusion

The historical argument presents science as the movement from the human effort to know what exists toward the deliberate production of new knowledge and artifacts. Its second-knower thesis highlights shared scrutiny and the need for a reality beyond private conviction. The critical task is to distinguish that productive epistemic proposal from claims that require independent evidence and from theological propositions that cannot be established by laboratory confirmation alone.

This version brings the argument into a single English academic structure while preserving the difference between **historical source**, **later canon**, and **editorial scientific assessment**.

## 9. Initial verified literature engagement: objectivity and the second knower

**External scholarship, distinct from the historical source.** The following publications have been checked against their publisher or editorial webpages on 22 October 2026. Their existence and relevance are verified; they are not presented as endorsing Zanabûn or the historical author's conclusions.

**Scientific objectivity.** The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy article *Scientific Objectivity* (first published 2014; substantively revised 2020) discusses objectivity as fidelity to facts, reduction of personal bias, and a feature of critical scientific-community practices. Its treatment of intersubjectivity offers a useful comparison to the second-knower thesis: social criticism can improve objectivity, but shared judgment is not itself sufficient proof of correspondence with reality. Source: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-objectivity/ .

**Reproducibility versus replicability.** The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2019), *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science*, distinguishes reproducing computational results with the same data and methods from replicating a scientific result through studies using their own data. This distinction supplies operational questions for the author's notion of a second knower: Is the second person independently measuring the phenomenon, repeating an analysis, or merely agreeing? These are not equivalent acts of confirmation. Publisher: https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25303/chapter/3 ; verified DOI: https://doi.org/10.17226/25303 .

**Proposed operationalization (editorial, not a claim in the historical source).** A narrow research protocol could classify second-knower episodes as (a) verbal agreement, (b) independent critique of reasoning, (c) reanalysis of shared observations, or (d) independent collection of new observations. The proposed hypothesis would be that (c) and (d), when well designed, identify certain errors that (a) misses. A future empirical study must define error classes, sampling, preregistered outcome measures, and independence criteria before treating this as a tested result.
